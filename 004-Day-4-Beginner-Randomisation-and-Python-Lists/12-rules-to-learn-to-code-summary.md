## The 12 Rules — Summary & Personal Commentary

---

## Rule 1 — Trick Your Brain with the 20-Minute Rule

### The Core Idea

Your brain is lazy. It thinks struggle = dying, so it tries to trick you into doing nothing. Trick it back: tell yourself you'll only do **20 minutes** (or 10, or 5). The initial push is the hardest part — like moving a heavy rock on a mountain. Once friction breaks, momentum takes over.

### What Angela Says

> "As humans, we have a lot of inertia."
>
> "If as soon as you get home, you slump on the sofa and switch on the TV, you've already lost that evening."
>
> "The moment you enter the door and change to a new environment is the most crucial moment."

### What I Think

This is 100% true from experience. The days I study best are when I get out of bed and **go straight to the laptop** — no phone, no breakfast, no living room first. Just 5-10 minutes of starting kills all friction. After that, I can do anything else and come back to keep going because I already started.

Doom-scrolling never stops because your brain loves the "just one more" loop. Use that same mechanism for studying, and you've won.

**💡 Key insight:** A line is better when it gets longer. Build the streak one 20-minute block at a time.

---

## Rule 2 — Code for a Purpose

### The Core Idea

If you're learning to code just to learn code, it'll be painfully difficult. You need **real purpose** — build something that solves an actual problem for you or someone else.

### What Angela Says

> "Here's the truth. If you are learning to code for the sake of learning to code, it'll be pretty difficult for you to get good at it."

### What I Think

Make things at your skill level that you can actually use and modify. As you level up, build deeper apps. That's why coding exists — to make life easier with computers. No purpose = no motivation = no progress.

---

## Rule 3 — There Is No "Perfect Language"

### The Core Idea

Don't obsess over which language to learn. Core concepts (loops, conditionals, functions) are the same everywhere. Syntax differences are minor.

### What Angela Says

> "If you want to hammer a nail, you should be using a hammer. If you want to fix your water pipes, you'll probably need a spanner."
>
> "The core programming concepts: loops, conditionals, functions, etc. they're all the same. The difference is mostly syntactical."

### What I Think

She says there's no perfect language. She's being diplomatic. Here's my take:

- **C** — mother of all languages
- **Python** — Swiss Army knife, solves almost anything
- **JavaScript** — never regretted learning it

Case-specific tools exist (Swift for iOS, Kotlin for Android), but the big three always top the charts. Learn Python first, branch out later. Don't fuss.

---

## Rule 4 — Understand What You're Writing

### The Core Idea

**Don't blindly copy code.** If you paste without understanding, you're not learning — you're typing. The moment something breaks (and it will), you'll have zero idea how to fix it.

### What Angela Says

> "The most common problem is a student who just copies the code in the tutorial and has no clue what any of it does."
>
> "Two-thirds of the way in, none of the things you're typing makes sense and you start seeing red all over the screen. Bugs. Loads of them. The last 3 hours were spent copying code and you learnt nothing other than maybe that coding sucks."
>
> "If you see a tutorial that jumps from beginner to advanced after line 3 or uses 'simply' too liberally — **leave that tutorial.**"
>
> "The key is ramping. You want to be stretched over and over again. If the ramp is too steep, you'll get lost. Too shallow, you'll get bored."

### What I Think

This might be the most important rule. I've been guilty of this — copy-pasting, watching tutorials at 2x, nodding along, then freezing when I try to build something alone.

**If you don't understand something, STOP.** Don't tell yourself "I'll get it later." You won't. Level 1 must be finished before level 2. Adjust speed based on material. Drop material that's wrong for your level.

Use speed controls liberally — 2x for familiar stuff, 0.5x for new concepts.

---

## Rule 5 — It's Okay to Not Know

### The Core Idea

The worst error you've encountered is one search away. Try to figure it out yourself first. Then Google it. Then Stack Overflow. The skill employers actually want is **thinking**, not memorization.

### What Angela Says

> "**The skill that most employers look for when recruiting is the ability to think.** Knowledge is valued in a world where information is hard to come by. Now, everyone has all the information at the tap of a mouse. **Information is losing value, the ability to think is the stock to buy.**"
>
> "The first option should always be trying to figure it out yourself. If you really can't figure it out, start with Google."

### What I Think

In the 1800s, only the rich had books and teachers. Information was valuable. Now information is free and infinite — **thinking** is the scarce resource. Don't be afraid to search. The best programmers do it constantly.

---

## Rule 6 — Be a Copycat (But Understand First)

### The Core Idea

Copying is fine — **if you understand what you're copying.** Books and docs are references. But if you want to _learn_, you have to **make something**.

### What Angela Says

> "Books are good as references. But if you want to learn, **make something.**"

### What I Think

Circles back to Rule 4. Copy → understand → modify → build your own version. That's the cycle.

---

## Rule 7 — Be Accountable

### The Core Idea

Show your work to someone. Have consequences for not showing up. Free things feel worthless because there's no cost.

### What Angela Says

> "Be accountable to someone. Show your work."
>
> "The biggest problem with online coding courses is the lack of accountability. What are the consequences of not doing your homework? Nothing. Nobody cares."
>
> "**People don't value things that don't have a value.**"
>
> "It was actually detrimental to students' learning to offer a free course. We all have hoarding tendencies. **There's always tomorrow, she says.**"

### What I Think

This one hit hard. I don't have a mentor. I don't have peers. I don't have a boss. It's just me.

But here's the thing — **I will care. I have to.**

That line about free courses is golden: _"If gold were free, it'd be less valuable than iron."_ Value comes from cost. Right now, the only currency I have is **time**. I invest time, I expect return. No bank account yet — that comes after I learn Python and get a job.

> "There's always tomorrow" is just a pretty way of saying "I don't want to do that today."

Ouch. But true.

---

## Rule 8 — Keep Learning

### The Core Idea

Programming evolves constantly. Stay relevant by continuously re-inventing yourself. Stagnation = obsolescence.

### What Angela Says

> "Programming will keep evolving. In order to stay relevant, you have to keep re-inventing yourself."
>
> "The world will keep moving. If you stay in one place, you'll eventually be left behind."

### What I Think

This reminded me of something my father told me as a kid:

> **Stale water goes bad.** 💧

We were by a mountain stream. He drank from it directly. I asked how he knew it was clean. He said because it flows straight from the mountain — constantly moving. All the dirt gets washed away. It stays clean **as long as it keeps flowing**. Stale water grows bacteria, algae, mold. It goes bad.

As a developer: **stale skills go bad.** If I stop learning, I fall behind. If I stay stagnant, I lose relevance. Keep the water moving.

Great resource mentioned: **learnxinyminutes.com** — short, clean reference sheets for picking up new languages once you know core concepts. If you can drive one car, you can drive any car (with a little practice 👌).

## Rule 9 — Play Foosball

### The Core Idea

When stuck on a bug — step away. Walk, sleep, play something. Your subconscious keeps working. 9/10 times, the solution appears when you stop staring at the screen.

Also: **code less, think more.** The easiest code to debug is the code you never wrote.

### What Angela Says

> "When you see real programmers working, they tend to look like this: _IF I SIT HERE LONG ENOUGH STARING AT THE SCREEN, MAYBE I WILL BE INSPIRED._"
>
> "Do you have a bug you can't work out? Sleep on it, play foosball, go for a walk. In 9 out of 10 cases, the solution will appear. In the remaining 1 out of 10 cases, you're just screwed." 😂
>
> "Code less, think more. The easiest code to get rid of is the code that was never written."

### What I Think

Best Hollywood programming movie is _Mr. Robot_. Everything else is fantasy garbage where people type at 200wpm while 3 monitors show scrolling matrix code.

This chapter reinforces the earlier rules. Take breaks. Think before you type. Don't brute-force your way through problems. Best code = no code (when possible).

## Rule 10 — Get a Mentor

### The Core Idea

Good mentors don't give answers — they ask questions that make **you** think. Find someone who practices the Socratic method.

### What Angela Says

> "Good mentors don't solve your problems, rather they practice the Socratic method of asking good questions that get you to think for yourself."
>
> "So don't get hung up on information. Learn to think instead."

### What I Think

Shortest section. Would love to have a mentor. For now, following the other 11 rules and treating this vault / my notes as my accountability system will have to do.

---

## Rule 11 — Get Into the Habit of Chunking

### The Core Idea

Face a mountain? Break it into rocks. Don't attack massive problems head-first. Identify parts, break into smaller pieces, solve each one.

### What Angela Says

> "The more that you break down problems and define the issue that you're trying to solve, the easier it is to package your code into bite-sized chunks. The simpler the chunk, the easier it is to tackle."

### What I Think

Exactly. This is how I should approach the 100-day course. Not "100 days of Python" (mountain), but "today I'm learning about lists" (rock). Then "today I'm building Rock Paper Scissors" (another rock). One chunk at a time.

---

## Rule 12 — Break Someone Else's Code

### The Core Idea

Learn from real codebases. Read line by line. Try to understand why decisions were made. Rewrite small projects yourself and compare.

### What Angela Says

> "There's a rule in programming that says 'never copy-paste code that you don't understand.'"
>
> "Look at other people's code, try to understand it line by line, see if you can make it better."

### What I Think

Coding is rinse and repeat. You get better by **doing**, and by **reading** what others did. Open-source projects are goldmines for this. Find a small library or tool you use, read the source, learn from it.

---

## 🎯 Final Takeaway

> _Trick yourself to start. Build real things. Understand deeply. Never stay still. Find accountability (even if it's just yourself). Break mountains into rocks. Code less, think more._

**Time to code.** 🔥
